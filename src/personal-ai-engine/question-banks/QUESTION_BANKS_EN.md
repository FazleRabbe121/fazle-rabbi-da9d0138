# QUESTION BANKS — AI INTERVIEW & ENGLISH ASSESSMENT CENTER
These question banks are starter content for the interview system. The AI should ask questions aloud, one at a time. The “Evaluator looks for” notes are private scoring guidance and must not be shown before the candidate answers.

## Common spoken-interview instruction (show before every interview)
**SPOKEN INTERVIEW — IMPORTANT:** You must answer aloud in English. Tap Start Interview and allow microphone access. Listen to the question and speak clearly in complete sentences. Do not type your answers in Spoken Mode. If you do not understand, ask: “Could you please repeat the question?” The system will transcribe your spoken answer. Use Written Practice only if you deliberately select that mode. Each level lasts at least five minutes.

## Default score rubric (100)
Role knowledge 25; situational judgement/problem-solving 25; grammar and vocabulary 20; clarity/fluency 20; professionalism/teamwork/safety 10. Suggested pass mark: 70/100, configurable. Critical safety failures can override a passing total.

# 1. WAITER

## Level 1 — Beginner (target 5–7 minutes)
1. Please introduce yourself and explain why you want to work as a waiter.  
   Evaluator looks for: understandable introduction, motivation, simple present tense, polite tone.
2. What are the main duties of a waiter before, during, and after service?  
   Looks for: preparing tables, menus, order taking, serving, clearing, cleanliness, teamwork.
3. How would you greet guests who have just arrived? Give the exact words you would use.  
   Looks for: warm greeting, eye contact described appropriately, polite language.
4. A guest asks for the menu and today’s special. What would you say?  
   Looks for: helpful response, accurate information, offer to explain dishes.
5. You did not hear a guest’s order clearly. What would you do?  
   Looks for: polite clarification, repeat-back, no guessing.
6. Why are personal hygiene and a clean uniform important in a restaurant?  
   Looks for: food safety, professional appearance, handwashing, clean uniform.
Follow-up if time remains: “What would you do first when you arrive for your shift?”

## Level 2 — Intermediate (target 8–10 minutes)
1. Several tables need service at once. How would you prioritise your tasks?  
   Looks for: urgency, order accuracy, communication, keeping guests informed.
2. A guest says their food is cold. Respond as if speaking directly to the guest.  
   Looks for: apology, empathy, prompt kitchen communication, realistic follow-up.
3. A guest mentions a food allergy. What steps must you take before confirming the order?  
   Looks for: never guess, check official allergen information, tell kitchen/supervisor, prevent cross-contact.
4. The wrong dish arrives at a table. How do you resolve it and update the guest?  
   Looks for: take responsibility, confirm correct order, coordinate with kitchen, follow up.
5. Recommend a suitable dish or drink without making the guest feel pressured.  
   Looks for: ask preferences, honest description, optional recommendation.
6. Explain how you communicate with kitchen staff during a busy service.  
   Looks for: concise details, table/order identification, confirmation, respectful teamwork.
Follow-up: “What would you say if the replacement meal will take longer than expected?”

## Level 3 — Advanced (target 10–15 minutes)
1. A reservation has been lost, the restaurant is full, and the guest is angry. Explain your response step by step.  
   Looks for: calm de-escalation, empathy, realistic options, supervisor escalation.
2. You suspect a dish was served to a guest with an allergy without proper checks. What do you do immediately?  
   Looks for: treat as urgent, alert manager/chef, follow emergency procedures, do not minimise risk.
3. A guest disputes the bill while another table needs urgent help. How do you prioritise and resolve both?  
   Looks for: assess urgency, communicate, verify bill, delegate/escalate.
4. A guest appears intoxicated and requests another alcoholic drink. What would you do?  
   Looks for: follow law and venue policy, refuse service respectfully when required, inform supervisor, protect safety.
5. A VIP asks for an unavailable dish while the kitchen is delayed. How do you manage expectations?  
   Looks for: transparent explanation, alternatives, no false promises, discreet service.
6. Describe a service mistake and how you would take responsibility and prevent recurrence.  
   Looks for: accountability, learning, corrective action, clear English.
Follow-up: “How would you brief your supervisor after the incident?”

# 2. BARTENDER

## Level 1 — Beginner (target 5–7 minutes)
1. Introduce yourself and explain why you want to work as a bartender.  
   Looks for: motivation, communication, customer focus.
2. What are the main duties before opening, during service, and at closing?  
   Looks for: mise en place, cleanliness, stock checks, service, cash/closing procedures.
3. What is the difference between shaking, stirring, and building a cocktail? Give an example of when each method is used.  
   Looks for: basic method knowledge and sensible examples.
4. What information should you confirm before preparing a guest’s drink?  
   Looks for: exact order, preferences, allergies where relevant, alcohol/non-alcohol choice, responsible service.
5. How do you keep a bar clean and organised during a busy shift?  
   Looks for: clean tools, safe glass handling, spill control, station organisation.
6. What would you say if you did not know the ingredients of a cocktail a guest ordered?  
   Looks for: honesty, checking recipe/menu, asking a colleague rather than guessing.
Follow-up: “Name three common base spirits and one cocktail made with each.”

## Level 2 — Intermediate (target 8–10 minutes)
1. Explain how you would prepare a classic cocktail consistently, from checking the recipe to serving it. Choose one example.  
   Looks for: measured recipe, correct glass/ice, method, garnish, presentation.
2. A guest says their cocktail is too sweet. How would you respond and offer a solution?  
   Looks for: listening, polite service recovery, balanced adjustment within recipe.
3. Explain the difference between a spirit, a liqueur, a vermouth, and a bitters product.  
   Looks for: accurate broad distinctions and examples.
4. How do you manage stock rotation, opened bottles, garnish freshness, and wastage?  
   Looks for: date labels, FIFO where appropriate, hygiene, inventory records.
5. A guest requests a drink but has an allergy or dietary restriction. What do you check?  
   Looks for: verify ingredients and cross-contact risks, consult official product info, never guess.
6. Describe how you would handle several drink orders while maintaining speed and accuracy.  
   Looks for: workflow, batching only where recipes allow, prioritisation, order checking.
Follow-up: “How would you explain a cocktail’s flavour profile to a guest?”

## Level 3 — Advanced (target 10–15 minutes)
1. Design a balanced signature cocktail for a specified flavour preference and explain base spirit, acidity, sweetness, dilution, texture, and garnish.  
   Looks for: balance, coherent recipe logic, clear explanation.
2. A guest appears intoxicated and insists on another alcoholic drink. Explain your response.  
   Looks for: responsible service, venue policy/local law, tact, supervisor escalation.
3. During a rush, a high-value bottle is missing and stock records do not match. How do you investigate and report it?  
   Looks for: check records, avoid accusations, follow inventory/security procedure.
4. A cocktail is sent back because it differs from the standard recipe. How do you diagnose and correct the issue?  
   Looks for: verify spec, measurements, dilution, ice, glass, garnish, communication.
5. How would you create a seasonal cocktail menu that controls cost and reduces waste?  
   Looks for: ingredient overlap, seasonality, cost/yield, staff training, guest appeal.
6. Explain how you would train a junior bartender to deliver consistent recipes and safe service.  
   Looks for: demonstrations, recipe cards, supervised practice, feedback, safety.
Follow-up: “What measurable checks would you use to monitor beverage cost and consistency?”

# 3. BARISTA

## Level 1 — Beginner (target 5–7 minutes)
1. Introduce yourself and explain why you want to work as a barista.  
   Looks for: motivation, customer service, basic spoken English.
2. What is the difference between espresso, Americano, cappuccino, and latte?  
   Looks for: basic drink construction and milk/espresso differences.
3. Describe the basic steps for preparing an espresso.  
   Looks for: clean portafilter, dose, distribute/tamp appropriately, extract, check result.
4. Why should the grinder and coffee equipment be kept clean?  
   Looks for: hygiene, flavour consistency, equipment care.
5. How would you greet a customer and confirm a takeaway coffee order?  
   Looks for: polite greeting, size, milk, temperature, takeaway/dine-in, repeat-back.
6. What would you do if you made the wrong drink?  
   Looks for: acknowledge, remake according to policy, communicate politely.
Follow-up: “What is the purpose of milk steaming?”

## Level 2 — Intermediate (target 8–10 minutes)
1. Espresso is running much too quickly. What variables would you check and adjust?  
   Looks for: grind size, dose, distribution/tamp, yield/time, controlled adjustment.
2. Explain how to steam milk for a latte and a cappuccino.  
   Looks for: aeration, whirlpool/texture, temperature control, microfoam differences.
3. A customer says the coffee tastes bitter or sour. How would you investigate?  
   Looks for: extraction, grind, dose/yield, water, bean freshness, avoid changing everything at once.
4. How do you keep recipes consistent during a busy service?  
   Looks for: recipe standards, scales/timers where used, grinder checks, cleaning and communication.
5. How would you handle a customer who is unhappy with a milk alternative or allergy-related request?  
   Looks for: verify product, cross-contact procedures, clean tools, no unsupported promises.
6. Explain FIFO, coffee bean storage, and why freshness matters.  
   Looks for: sealed storage, protection from heat/moisture/odours, date awareness.
Follow-up: “What information would you record when dialling in a new coffee?”

## Level 3 — Advanced (target 10–15 minutes)
1. Describe a systematic espresso dial-in process for a new coffee and explain how you evaluate taste.  
   Looks for: dose, yield, time, grind adjustment, tasting, one-variable-at-a-time method.
2. A machine’s espresso pressure or temperature appears unstable during service. What is your safe troubleshooting process?  
   Looks for: check indicators and routine maintenance, stop unsafe use, report to technician; no unauthorised repairs.
3. Develop a workflow for 12 mixed hot and iced drink orders while maintaining quality and allergen controls.  
   Looks for: ticket organisation, sequencing, clear labelling, separate tools where needed, final checks.
4. Explain how roast level, origin, processing, grind size, water, and extraction affect the cup.  
   Looks for: nuanced but clear explanation, avoid overgeneralisation.
5. How would you train a team to improve latte-art consistency without slowing service excessively?  
   Looks for: milk texture, pouring basics, practice plan, standards, realistic workflow.
6. How would you reduce waste and monitor coffee quality across several shifts?  
   Looks for: waste log, dial-in records, recipe compliance, cleaning schedule, staff calibration.
Follow-up: “What would you do if a recipe change improved speed but reduced flavour quality?”

# 4. MAINTENANCE ELECTRICIAN
Safety note: Questions are for interview assessment only. Real electrical work must be done by qualified, authorised personnel under applicable local regulations and safe isolation procedures.

## Level 1 — Entry / Basic Operational (target 5–7 minutes)
1. Explain the safe isolation and Lockout/Tagout (LOTO) steps before working on a motorised machine. What PPE may be required for arc-flash risk?  
   Evaluator looks for: notify/prepare, shut down, isolate all energy sources, lock/tag, verify zero energy with an appropriate tester (“test before touch”), suitable risk-assessed PPE.
2. Differentiate overload and short circuit. How does a thermal-magnetic circuit breaker respond?  
   Looks for: overload is excess current over time; thermal element trips; short circuit is a low-impedance fault and magnetic element trips rapidly.
3. How would you diagnose an open circuit versus a short to ground on a de-energised circuit using a multimeter?  
   Looks for: safe isolation and verification first; appropriate continuity/resistance tests; near-zero resistance to ground may indicate fault, interpreted in circuit context.
4. Compare single-phase 230 V and three-phase systems and describe typical uses.  
   Looks for: basic supply differences, domestic/light loads versus many commercial/industrial motors and equipment.
5. What does an RCD do, and why is it different from an overcurrent circuit breaker?  
   Looks for: detects residual current imbalance/earth leakage; breaker protects against overload/short circuit; complementary functions.
Follow-up: “What would you do if you could not prove a circuit was isolated?”

## Level 2 — Intermediate Troubleshooting (target 8–10 minutes)
1. A three-phase fan motor runs in reverse after replacement. What might cause it, and what safe process should a qualified electrician follow?  
   Looks for: phase sequence changed; isolate/LOTO, verify and correct according to procedure, controlled rotation test.
2. Compare a control relay and a magnetic contactor. When is each appropriate?  
   Looks for: relay for control/signalling and smaller loads; contactor for frequent switching of larger loads such as motors, selected to rating.
3. Explain a star-delta starter sequence and why electrical/mechanical interlocks are necessary.  
   Looks for: star start reduces winding voltage/current; transition to delta for running; interlocks prevent simultaneous contactor closure.
4. A conveyor trips after about 45 minutes. Outline a diagnostic plan.  
   Looks for: operator history/fault codes, overload settings, ventilation, bearings, voltage/current imbalance, measured current, thermal inspection by qualified personnel.
5. Explain IP ratings and what should be considered for an outdoor isolator near washdown.  
   Looks for: first digit solids, second liquids; choose rating based on actual exposure and local requirements, e.g. appropriate water-jet protection; do not specify without checking conditions.
Follow-up: “How would you document findings and confirm the repair?”

## Level 3 — Advanced Systems / Technical Leadership (target 10–15 minutes)
1. Explain how a VFD controls motor speed using PWM and discuss risks from long motor cables.  
   Looks for: AC-DC-AC conversion, variable frequency/voltage, switching, reflected-wave/insulation stress; suitable filters/cable practices per manufacturer.
2. A facility has a lagging power factor of 0.72. Explain impacts and how an APFC solution would be assessed.  
   Looks for: utility penalties where applicable, higher current/losses and reduced capacity; load survey, capacitor stages/controller, harmonic assessment and detuned reactors where needed.
3. PLCs reboot randomly and sensors give inaccurate readings. How would you investigate possible harmonics, grounding, and EMI?  
   Looks for: power-quality measurements, grounding/shielding checks, cable separation/routing, isolation/noise mitigation by qualified staff.
4. Propose a preventive maintenance schedule for an 11 kV/415 V dry-type transformer.  
   Looks for: risk/manufacturer-based inspections, cooling/temperature, connections/thermography, insulation and protection tests by authorised specialists at approved intervals.
5. Explain a Buchholz relay and the difference between alarm and trip.  
   Looks for: oil-immersed conservator transformer; gas accumulation may alarm; rapid oil surge from severe internal fault may trip, per design.
Follow-up: “What would make you stop work and escalate rather than continue testing?”

# 5. PLUMBER
Safety note: Technical answers must follow local codes, manufacturer instructions, and qualified-person requirements. Numeric standards vary by jurisdiction and system design; the evaluator must not treat every example value as universal law.

## Level 1 — Entry / Basic Operational (target 5–7 minutes)
1. Compare PVC, CPVC, and copper pipes and describe typical applications and temperature limitations.  
   Looks for: material properties, cold/drainage versus suitable hot/cold systems depending on certification, copper durability; check manufacturer/local code.
2. What is the purpose of a P-trap, and how can its water seal be lost?  
   Looks for: blocks sewer gases; siphoning/evaporation; venting balances pressure.
3. Describe the steps and safety precautions for making a sound soldered copper joint.  
   Looks for: square cut/deburr, clean surfaces, suitable flux/lead-free solder, heat fitting correctly, fire protection, inspection.
4. Why is correct slope important for horizontal drainage?  
   Looks for: sufficient flow to carry solids; too little can cause deposits, incorrect excessive slope may leave solids behind; follow local code.
5. How would you conduct a pressure test on a new domestic water installation?  
   Looks for: isolate as required, cap outlets, gauge, fill/vent safely, test to local code/manufacturer pressure and duration; never assume one universal pressure.
Follow-up: “What would you do if pressure dropped during a test?”

## Level 2 — Intermediate Troubleshooting (target 8–10 minutes)
1. Explain the risk of a cross-connection and how an RPZ backflow preventer reduces contamination risk.  
   Looks for: potable/non-potable connection, back-siphonage/backpressure, check valves and relief mechanism, required inspection/testing.
2. A gas-fired water heater cycles frequently and produces insufficient hot water. What would you check and when would you call a specialist?  
   Looks for: thermostat, sediment, flame sensor/thermocouple, venting/combustion air, dip tube; gas safety and authorised work.
3. Describe the main parts and maintenance checks of a sewage ejector pump system.  
   Looks for: basin, pump/impeller, vent, check/isolation valves, float switches, safe electrical isolation and hygiene.
4. Explain thermal expansion in a closed hot-water system and the role of an expansion tank.  
   Looks for: heated water expands; closed system pressure rises; correctly sized/pre-charged tank installed per design.
5. A commercial kitchen drain is severely blocked by grease. Explain your safe diagnostic and clearance plan.  
   Looks for: correct cleanout access, suitable mechanical or hydro-jetting equipment by trained staff, PPE, grease trap considerations, post-clearance verification.
Follow-up: “How would you confirm the blockage is fully cleared?”

## Level 3 — Advanced Systems / Technical Leadership (target 10–15 minutes)
1. Explain how you would size a booster pump and hydro-pneumatic tank for a multi-storey commercial building.  
   Looks for: demand/fixture units and occupancy, static head, friction losses, required residual pressure, pump duty/standby and tank cycling calculations.
2. Explain water hammer, its possible damage, and design-level mitigation.  
   Looks for: pressure wave from rapid velocity change; suitable arrestors, valve selection, velocity/control, pipe supports.
3. Develop a hot-water maintenance strategy to reduce Legionella risk while preventing scalding.  
   Looks for: risk assessment, monitored temperatures, circulation, flushing, mixing valves, documented procedures consistent with local public-health guidance.
4. Distinguish greywater and blackwater systems and describe safe separation and reuse controls.  
   Looks for: source differences, separate labelled pipework, treatment/disinfection, cross-connection prevention, local code.
5. A water softener output fails a hardness test. Describe a systematic diagnostic sequence.  
   Looks for: confirm raw-water baseline, salt/brine issues, controller/regeneration settings, injector blockage, resin condition, bypass/internal leaks.
Follow-up: “What measurements and service records would you keep?”

# 6. ENGLISH LEVEL ASSESSMENT — CEFR A1 TO C2
The assessment should adapt to the selected target level and ask questions one at a time. Evaluate grammar, vocabulary, comprehension, coherence, fluency, task completion, and interaction. Spoken answers require audio transcription. Do not assign an official CEFR certificate; report an estimated level and confidence.

## A1 — Beginner (5–7 minutes)
1. What is your name, and where are you from?
2. What do you do every day?
3. Describe your family or a person you know.
4. What food and drinks do you like?
5. Tell me about your usual working day.
Evaluator: simple phrases, basic present tense, common vocabulary, understandable short answers.

## A2 — Elementary (5–7 minutes)
1. Tell me about your last weekend.
2. Describe your current job or a job you would like to do.
3. Explain how you travel to work.
4. What would you say if a customer asked for help?
5. Describe a problem you had and how you solved it.
Evaluator: routine exchanges, simple past/future, connected short sentences, familiar topics.

## B1 — Intermediate (5–7 minutes)
1. Describe a challenging situation at work and how you handled it.
2. Explain the advantages and disadvantages of working in a team.
3. What would you do if you disagreed with a colleague?
4. Describe a goal you achieved and how you achieved it.
5. Give your opinion on why good customer service matters.
Evaluator: connected discourse, reasons/examples, mostly controlled grammar, practical independence.

## B2 — Upper Intermediate (5–7 minutes)
1. Describe a workplace problem and compare two possible solutions.
2. Do you think customer expectations have changed in recent years? Explain.
3. Explain how you would handle a misunderstanding with a manager.
4. Discuss the benefits and risks of using AI at work.
5. Defend an opinion while acknowledging an opposing view.
Evaluator: clear detailed argument, range of vocabulary, complex structures, interaction and nuance.

## C1 — Advanced (5–7 minutes)
1. Analyse a difficult professional decision and its wider consequences.
2. Explain how you would persuade a team to adopt a process improvement.
3. Discuss a complex ethical issue in the workplace, including competing priorities.
4. Summarise a technical topic for both expert and non-expert audiences.
5. Respond to an unexpected follow-up question and refine your argument.
Evaluator: flexible, fluent, well-structured speech, nuanced vocabulary, accurate complex grammar.

## C2 — Proficient (5–7 minutes)
1. Give a nuanced argument about a complex workplace issue and address counterarguments.
2. Interpret an ambiguous professional scenario and explain multiple plausible readings.
3. Reformulate a technical explanation for three audiences with different levels of knowledge.
4. Critically evaluate a policy or proposal, distinguishing evidence from assumptions.
5. Respond to a challenging objection and synthesise a balanced conclusion.
Evaluator: precise nuance, idiomatic control, sophisticated coherence, flexible reformulation, subtle meaning.

## English test result
Provide estimated CEFR level A1–C2, skill breakdown, examples of grammar/vocabulary errors, corrected versions, strengths, improvement plan, and confidence level. If the test samples too little evidence, return “Insufficient evidence—continue assessment” rather than overclaiming.

# Interview flow requirements
1. Explain spoken mode and microphone requirement.
2. Confirm consent for audio capture/transcription.
3. Show role and level, duration, and assessment criteria.
4. Ask question 1; capture answer; save it.
5. Ask an appropriate follow-up if needed; save answer.
6. Continue until minimum duration and required skill coverage are reached.
7. Generate score, PASS/FAIL, explanations and recommendations.
8. Save a permanent attempt record and show a result summary.
